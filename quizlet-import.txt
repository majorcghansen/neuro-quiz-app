Aristotle	Skeptic who did NOT believe the brain controls behavior — cited headless chickens/insects still moving.
Galen	Physician to gladiators; first to link brain/head injury to behavioral change.
Fritz & Hitzig (1870)	Stimulated dog brains with electricity; established the brain is electrically excitable with regional specificity, replicable across species.
Bartholow & Mary Rafferty	First evidence electrical brain stimulation controls behavior in a living, awake human; contributed to the AMA banning non-consensual human experimentation.
John Flynn	Stimulated cat hypothalamus; different sites produced affective defense vs. quiet biting attack — complex, goal-directed behaviors, not just twitches.
Wilder Penfield	Stimulated awake epilepsy patients' exposed cortex; mapped sensory/motor regions and evoked specific memories/emotions.
Michael Gazzaniga	Split-brain research — studied patients after corpus callosotomy (severed corpus callosum), revealing hemispheric communication deficits.
Contralateral	Opposite side of the body/brain.
Ipsilateral	Same side of the body/brain.
Corpus callosum	Band of fibers connecting the left and right cerebral hemispheres.
Soma	The cell body of a neuron.
Nissl stain	Stains the endoplasmic reticulum; since ER is concentrated in the cell body, this effectively stains cell bodies (somas).
Hodgkin & Huxley	Measured the electrical properties of the squid giant axon; won the Nobel Prize (1963) for describing the action potential.
Resting potential	The baseline membrane voltage of a neuron at rest, about −70 mV (inside negative relative to outside).
Action potential	The stereotyped voltage spike: depolarization → repolarization → hyperpolarization → return to rest.
Nernst equation	Predicts the equilibrium voltage for a single freely-moving ion given its concentration inside vs. outside the membrane.
Sodium-potassium pump	Active transport protein that pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle, powered by ATP; maintains the negative resting potential.
Threshold	The membrane voltage (~−55 mV) that must be reached to trigger a full action potential.
Voltage-gated channel	A membrane channel that opens/closes based on membrane voltage; each type is ion-specific (e.g., Na⁺ or K⁺).
Depolarization	Na⁺ influx causes the membrane voltage to rise (become less negative/more positive).
Repolarization	K⁺ efflux causes the membrane voltage to fall back down toward negative.
Hyperpolarization	A brief overshoot below resting potential after repolarization, before the Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores balance.
Refractory period	~2 ms window after an action potential during which a neuron cannot fire another (channels still resetting).
All-or-none law	Every action potential in a given neuron is identical in size, shape, and speed — there is no 'bigger' or 'smaller' spike.
Myelin (CNS)	Produced by oligodendrocytes; insulates axons and speeds conduction.
Myelin (PNS)	Produced by Schwann cells; insulates peripheral axons.
Nodes of Ranvier	Unmyelinated gaps between myelin segments where the action potential actually regenerates (saltatory conduction).
Synapse	The space (cleft) between the presynaptic and postsynaptic neuron where neurotransmission occurs (~10 nm wide).
Ligand-gated channel	A channel that opens when a specific molecule (ligand), such as a neurotransmitter, binds to it.
EPSP	Excitatory postsynaptic potential — a depolarizing response (e.g., Na⁺ influx) that moves the cell closer to threshold.
IPSP	Inhibitory postsynaptic potential — a hyperpolarizing response (e.g., K⁺ efflux or Cl⁻ influx) that moves the cell further from threshold.
Ionotropic receptor	Neurotransmitter directly opens an ion channel — fast onset, short-lived effect.
Metabotropic receptor	Neurotransmitter triggers a second-messenger cascade inside the cell — slower onset, but longer-lasting; can alter gene transcription.
Glutamate	The primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the CNS; produces EPSPs via Na⁺ influx.
GABA	The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS; produces IPSPs via K⁺ efflux.
Acetylcholine	Neurotransmitter used at the neuromuscular junction; the first neurotransmitter discovered.
Quanta	Neurotransmitter is released in fixed packets (one vesicle's worth) — release is always an integer multiple of one quantum.
Dale's Law	Each neuron generally releases one primary type of neurotransmitter at its terminals (with some exceptions releasing a fixed ratio of multiple transmitters).
Reuptake	Transporters on the presynaptic cell pull released neurotransmitter back in via endocytosis, recycling it into vesicles.
Interoception	The monitoring of internal body state; linked to the insula's role in voluntary autonomic control.
Biofeedback	Techniques that let humans consciously alter normally-involuntary functions like heart rate.
Insular cortex (insula)	Brain region implicated in autonomic control/interoception and in nicotine addiction (damage makes quitting easier).
CNS	Central nervous system — the brain and spinal cord.
PNS	Peripheral nervous system — the nerves connecting the CNS to the rest of the body.
Cerebellum	The 'little brain'; packs in more neurons than the entire cerebrum.
Glia-to-neuron ratio	Newer isotropic fractionation counting shows glia and neurons are roughly equal overall (not the classic 10:1 estimate), varying widely by region.
Multiple sclerosis	A demyelinating disease in which myelin-forming glial cells die, exposing axons and impairing sensory/motor function.
Dendritic spines	Small protrusions on dendrites (thin, stubby, or mushroom-shaped) that are plastic and form the basis of most excitatory synaptic connections.
Absolute refractory period	The period right after an action potential when no stimulation, regardless of strength, can trigger another one.
Relative refractory period	The period after the absolute refractory period when a stronger-than-usual stimulus is needed to trigger an action potential.
Otto Loewi	Performed the 1921 frog-heart 'dream' experiment proving chemical synaptic transmission; shared the 1936 Nobel Prize with Henry Dale.
Vagusstoff	Loewi's name for the chemical (later identified as acetylcholine) released by vagus nerve stimulation.
Temporal summation	Rapid repeated firing at the same synapse whose postsynaptic effects stack before decaying.
Spatial summation	Combining postsynaptic potentials from multiple synapses at different locations.
Presynaptic facilitation	An axoaxonic synapse effect that increases neurotransmitter release.
Presynaptic inhibition	An axoaxonic synapse effect that decreases neurotransmitter release.
Astrocytes	Glial cells that ensheathe synapses/blood vessels, regulate extracellular K+, and promote synaptogenesis (e.g., via thrombospondin).
Epileptogenesis	The process by which a healthy brain transforms into a seizure-prone brain.
Gap junctions	Direct, bidirectional electrical synapses formed by aligned connexons between two neurons.
Glycine	The major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brainstem and spinal cord; blocked by the poison strychnine.
Agonist	A substance that mimics or enhances a neurotransmitter's effect at its receptor.
Antagonist	A substance that blocks or decreases a neurotransmitter's effect at its receptor.
Affinity	A drug's tendency to bind closely to its target receptor.
Efficacy	A drug's ability to produce the desired effect for a condition.
Potency	A drug's ability to induce an effect relative to other drugs.
Pharmacokinetics	What the body does to a drug — absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination.
Pharmacodynamics	What a drug does to the body — its effects and mechanisms.
Placebo effect	Improvement due to belief in a treatment, not the treatment itself.
Endocannabinoids	Lipid-derived transmitters (e.g., anandamide) made on-demand and not stored in vesicles.
Retrograde neurotransmitter	A neurotransmitter released by the postsynaptic neuron that acts back on the presynaptic axon.
Opioid receptors	Mu, delta, and kappa receptors that respond to endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins) and drugs like morphine.
THC	δ-9-tetrahydrocannabinol — the active psychoactive ingredient in cannabis; acts on cannabinoid receptors.
Doogie mice	Tsien (2000) transgenic mice with enhanced NMDA receptor function showed better object recognition, memory, and maze-solving.
Curare	A poison that blocks nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing paralysis.
Bystander stress study (Mychasiuk et al., 2011)	Indirect prenatal stress (a stressed cage-mate) altered dendritic length/spine density in rat offspring brains, with females more affected than males.
Spatial vs. temporal summation (firing rate limit)	Temporal summation is limited by one neuron's max firing rate (~200 Hz); spatial summation isn't, since it combines input from many different presynaptic neurons at once.
Axon hillock	The site where the axon meets the soma; graded potentials decay with distance, so synapses closer to the hillock have more influence on whether an action potential fires.
Synaptic cleft (size)	The gap between presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes, about 20–50 nanometers wide.
Exocytosis	The process by which synaptic vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
G-protein coupled receptor	A metabotropic receptor type; the G-protein relays/amplifies the signal inside the cell, can modulate nearby ion channels, enzymes, or gene expression.
Myasthenia Gravis	Autoimmune disease where antibodies attack postsynaptic ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing progressive muscle weakness; no cure.
Substantia nigra	"Black substance" — a pigmented, dopaminergic-neuron-rich part of the basal ganglia; its degeneration causes Parkinson's disease.
L-DOPA	A dopamine precursor that can cross the blood-brain barrier (unlike dopamine itself); used to treat Parkinson's disease, though tolerance develops over time.
Resting tremor	A tremor that occurs when the affected body part is at rest (not moving); characteristic of Parkinson's disease, distinct from intention tremor.
Huntington's disease	Autosomal dominant genetic disorder causing chorea (excessive involuntary movement); caused by death of inhibitory medium spiny neurons in the striatum.
Medium spiny neurons	Inhibitory, GABAergic neurons of the caudate/putamen (striatum); their death causes the excess movement seen in Huntington's disease.
Chorea	Involuntary, irregular, dance-like movements; a hallmark symptom of Huntington's disease (hemiballism is a severe throwing-like form).
Botulinum toxin (Botox)	Blocks ACh release from presynaptic terminals, causing flaccid paralysis; effects last ~3 months.
Black widow spider venom	Promotes/over-stimulates ACh release from presynaptic terminals, causing rigid paralysis (opposite mechanism to Botox).
Muscarine	Selectively activates metabotropic ACh receptors; used with nicotine to pharmacologically distinguish ionotropic from metabotropic ACh receptor effects.
Acetylcholinesterase	The enzyme that breaks down ACh into choline + acetate in the synaptic cleft; blocking it prolongs ACh signaling.
SSRI (Prozac) mechanism	Selectively blocks the serotonin reuptake transporter, keeping serotonin in the synaptic cleft longer and amplifying serotonergic signaling.
Typical vs. atypical antipsychotics	Typical (e.g., Haldol) mainly block/decrease dopamine; atypical (e.g., Risperdal) work more on serotonin.
Novocaine	A cocaine derivative used for local anesthesia; works by reducing Na⁺ channel permeability, unlike cocaine's psychoactive reuptake-blocking effect.
